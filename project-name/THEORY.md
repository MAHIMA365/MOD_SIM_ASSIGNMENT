# Theory and mathematics

## 1. Problem formulation

Passengers arrive at a bus halt and wait until a boarding bay is available. This project compares two queueing configurations: one boarding bay, modelled as an M/M/1 queue, and two boarding bays, modelled as an M/M/2 queue with one shared queue. In Kendall notation, the first M represents Poisson arrivals (equivalently, exponentially distributed inter-arrival times), the second M represents exponentially distributed service times, and the final number is the number of servers.

The simulation will generate passenger arrival times and boarding service durations, then track the queue and server states as events occur. The configurations will be compared under selected arrival and service rates. Quantities of interest are the mean passenger waiting time in the queue, the time-average queue length, passenger throughput, and server utilisation. The analytical steady-state equations below provide reference values for verification; they are not experimental results.

## 2. Assumptions

1. **Poisson arrivals and exponential inter-arrival times.** Passenger arrivals form a homogeneous Poisson process with constant rate \(\lambda\) while a scenario is running. Consequently, independent inter-arrival times are exponentially distributed with rate \(\lambda\). This assumption is a tractable baseline; real passenger arrivals may vary over time or arrive in groups.
2. **Exponential service times.** Boarding service durations are independent and exponentially distributed with rate \(\mu\) per bay. Both bays in the two-bay scenario use the same service rate.
3. **First-come, first-served (FCFS).** Passengers join the end of the waiting line and, when a bay becomes available, the passenger who has waited longest is served next.
4. **Unlimited waiting capacity and no abandonment.** The model imposes no finite limit on the queue, and passengers do not leave before service. These are simplifying assumptions, not claims about every bus halt.
5. **Independent servers and one shared queue in M/M/2.** The two bays are identical, operate independently, and each serves at most one passenger at a time. All waiting passengers join one shared FCFS queue; the next passenger is assigned to the first bay that becomes available.
6. **Independent random inputs.** Arrival intervals and service durations are mutually independent, and successive values within each process are independent.
7. **Steady-state stability.** Analytical steady-state measures are finite only when the arrival rate is below the total service capacity: \(\lambda < \mu\) for one bay and \(\lambda < 2\mu\) for two identical bays. Simulated scenarios intended for steady-state comparison must satisfy the corresponding condition. Transient or overloaded scenarios may be studied separately but should not be interpreted using steady-state formulas.

## 3. Model equations

Let \(U\) denote a random draw from the continuous uniform distribution on \((0,1)\). Exponential random variates can be generated by inverse transformation:

$$
\Delta A = -\frac{\ln U_A}{\lambda},
\qquad
S = -\frac{\ln U_S}{\mu}.
$$

Here, \(\Delta A\) is an inter-arrival time, \(S\) is a service duration, and \(U_A\) and \(U_S\) are independent uniform random draws for arrivals and service, respectively. If rates are specified in passengers per unit time, the generated durations are in the corresponding unit of time. For example, rates per minute produce durations in minutes. The random-number generator should use a recorded seed to make runs reproducible.

### M/M/1: one boarding bay

For arrival rate \(\lambda\) and service rate \(\mu\), utilisation is

$$
\rho_1 = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}, \qquad 0 \leq \rho_1 < 1.
$$

The stability condition is \(\lambda < \mu\). In steady state, the mean waiting time **in the queue** and mean queue length are

$$
W_{q,1} = \frac{\rho_1}{\mu-\lambda}
= \frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)},
\qquad
L_{q,1} = \lambda W_{q,1}
= \frac{\rho_1^2}{1-\rho_1}.
$$

### M/M/2: two boarding bays with a shared queue

Let \(c=2\) be the number of identical bays and let \(a=\lambda/\mu\) be the offered traffic, measured in Erlangs (a dimensionless quantity). The utilisation of each bay is

$$
\rho_2 = \frac{\lambda}{c\mu} = \frac{\lambda}{2\mu},
\qquad 0 \leq \rho_2 < 1.
$$

The stability condition is \(\lambda < c\mu=2\mu\). The Erlang C probability that an arriving passenger must wait is

$$
P_{\mathrm{wait}}
=
\left(\frac{a^c}{c!(1-\rho_2)}\right)P_0,
\qquad
P_0 =
\left[
\sum_{n=0}^{c-1}\frac{a^n}{n!}
+ \frac{a^c}{c!(1-\rho_2)}
\right]^{-1}.
$$

Here \(P_0\) is the steady-state probability that both bays are idle (the system is empty). The mean queue waiting time and mean queue length are

$$
W_{q,2} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{wait}}}{c\mu-\lambda},
\qquad
L_{q,2} = \lambda W_{q,2}.
$$

For a stable system with no lost or abandoning passengers, the steady-state throughput is \(\lambda\) passengers per unit time. The expected fraction of time each bay is busy is \(\rho_1\) for M/M/1 and \(\rho_2\) for M/M/2. In simulation, throughput and utilisation will be estimated over a stated observation period; finite-run estimates need not equal their steady-state values exactly.

## 4. Numerical method

The model will use **next-event time-advance discrete-event simulation**. Simulation time advances directly to the earliest scheduled event rather than in fixed time steps. For each run, the simulator will generate the next passenger arrival and the service completion time for each busy bay. It will select the event with the earliest time, update the clock and system state, record any required statistics, and schedule subsequent events.

When an arrival occurs, the passenger enters service immediately if a bay is free; otherwise, the passenger joins the end of the shared FCFS queue. When a service completion occurs, the passenger departs; if the queue is non-empty, its first passenger begins service at that bay. In the two-bay model, each bay has its own service-completion event, while both draw waiting passengers from the same queue.

Arrival and service durations will be generated from independent pseudorandom uniform variates using the inverse-transform equations above. A fixed, recorded seed supports reproducibility. One boarding-bay and two boarding-bay scenarios will be run with matching arrival and per-bay service-rate inputs where comparisons require like-for-like conditions.

Because stochastic estimates vary from run to run, each scenario will use multiple independent replications with separate random streams or seeds. The number of replications, any warm-up period, and the observation duration will be specified and justified when the experiment is designed. Results will be summarised across replications; a confidence interval for a replication-level mean can be calculated as

$$
\bar{X} \ \pm\ t_{R-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\frac{s_X}{\sqrt{R}},
$$

where \(X_r\) is a measured quantity from replication \(r\), \(\bar{X}\) is its sample mean over \(R\) replications, \(s_X\) is the sample standard deviation across those replications, \(t_{R-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\) is the corresponding Student's \(t\) critical value, and \(1-\alpha\) is the selected confidence level. The replication count and confidence level are not specified here and will be reported with the experiments.

## 6. Concepts applied

| Concept | Where it is used in this project |
|---|---|
| Queueing theory | M/M/1 and M/M/2 equations describe the single-bay and two-bay queue configurations and provide steady-state reference measures. |
| Stochastic processes | The Poisson arrival process and exponential service-time model represent random passenger arrivals and boarding durations. |
| Random variate generation | Inverse-transform sampling converts pseudorandom uniform draws into exponential inter-arrival times and service durations. |
| Discrete-event simulation | The simulator schedules arrivals and service completions and advances to the next event to update the queue and bay states. |
| Monte Carlo simulation | Repeated stochastic runs estimate waiting time, queue length, throughput, and utilisation under each scenario. |
| Law of large numbers | With more independent observations or replications under suitable conditions, sample averages are expected to stabilise around their model means. |
| Confidence intervals | Replication-level variability will be used to quantify uncertainty in estimated scenario means and comparisons. |
